Hospitalised road traffic injuries · Australia · 2011–2021

Every year, tens of thousands of Australians end up in hospital after a road crash.

In 2021, 39,505 people were admitted to hospital with injuries from road traffic crashes, up from 34,033 in 2011. Road deaths get most of the attention, but non-fatal injuries affect far more people. This story looks at how that number changed, who was hurt, and where.

How to read these charts

  • Counts, not rates. The figures are numbers of hospitalisations. They are not adjusted for population size or how much people travel, so bigger states and groups will naturally have more.
  • Counting rules changed. Victoria (from July 2012) and NSW (from June 2017) stopped counting patients treated only in emergency departments, which lowered their counts.
  • A hospitalisation is not a crash. One crash can lead to several hospitalisations, and injuries that don't need a hospital admission aren't counted.

Question 1 · National trend

How did hospitalisations change from 2011 to 2021?

Hospitalisations rose by 16% over the decade, peaked at 39,866 in 2019, then fell in 2020 before rising again.

The overall rise is steady but not continuous. Some of the pattern reflects counting changes rather than road safety: the drop in Victoria's counts after 2012 and in NSW's after 2017 make the national total look lower than it would otherwise be. The dip in 2020 coincides with the COVID-19 period, but these data alone can't show the cause.

Question 2 · Road users

Which road users drove the change?

Pedal cyclists had the biggest increase (+2,772 hospitalisations), followed by car drivers and motorcyclists. Pedestrian hospitalisations fell.

Switch to change in share to see which groups became a larger or smaller part of all road traffic hospitalisations. A group can grow in number while shrinking as a share, because the total also grew.

Question 3 · States and territories

How did each state and territory change?

Queensland had the largest rise, from 6,322 in 2011 to 10,447 in 2021. NSW was the only jurisdiction lower in 2021 than in 2011, partly because of its 2017 counting change.

Each panel has its own vertical scale, starting at zero, so you can compare the shape of each trend. Don't compare the heights of lines between panels: NSW and the Northern Territory have very different populations, and these are raw counts.

Dashed lines mark counting changes: Victoria (2012) and NSW (2017). Hover or tab to a panel to compare its 2011 and 2021 counts.

Question 4 · Age and sex

Who was hospitalised, by age and sex?

Almost twice as many males as females were hospitalised. Males aged 40–64 were the largest group in 2021 (8,702), and hospitalisations among older Australians grew.

Switch between 2011 and 2021 to see how the age profile shifted. The age bands are different widths: 40–64 covers 25 years while 17–25 covers 9, so wider bands naturally contain more people.

Question 5 · First Nations Peoples

Hospitalisations of First Nations Peoples

Hospitalisations of First Nations Peoples rose from 1,150 in 2011 to 2,214 in 2021, with a peak of 2,290 in 2020. Motorcyclists grew from 16% to 23% of these hospitalisations.

The right-hand chart compares the mix of road users with the national mix, as percentages rather than counts. Road-user groups were combined in the national data to match the First Nations categories. These are descriptive patterns; the data don't explain why they occur.

Key takeaways

A growing burden

Road traffic hospitalisations rose 16% between 2011 and 2021, from 34,033 to 39,505, despite counting changes that lowered the numbers in two states.

Cyclists and drivers

Pedal cyclists, car drivers and motorcyclists account for most of the increase. Pedestrian hospitalisations fell.

Who is affected

About two in three hospitalisations are males. People aged 40–64 and over 65 make up a growing share, and motorcyclists are a larger share of First Nations hospitalisations.

What these data can't tell us

  • Risk: the counts are not adjusted for population or travel, so they don't show who is most at risk per person or per kilometre.
  • Causes: the data describe patterns, not why they happened.
  • Severity: a hospitalisation can be a short stay or a long one; this story counts them equally.
